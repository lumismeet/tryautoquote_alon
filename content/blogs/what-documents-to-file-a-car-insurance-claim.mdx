To file a car insurance claim you generally need four things: proof of what happened, proof of the damage, your policy details, and a record of everyone you deal with. In practice that means the other driver's insurance and contact information, photos of the damage and the scene, a police report or case number when there is one, your own policy number, and any repair estimates or bills. The exact list shifts depending on whether the claim is a collision, a theft or weather loss, or an injury, but the backbone is the same. Having it ready before you call speeds the claim and protects you if the payout is ever disputed. Here is exactly what to gather, in what order, and why each piece matters.

## Gather Documentation at the Scene First

The strongest claim is built in the first hour, while the evidence is still in front of you. If you are in an accident, document everything before cars are moved and memories fade. Take photos from several angles showing the damage to every vehicle, the position of the cars, the road and any skid marks, traffic signs or signals, and the overall scene. Wide shots that establish where everything sat are as valuable as close-ups of the dents.

Then collect the details you cannot recreate later. Get the other driver's name, phone number, address, insurance company, and policy number, along with the license plate and the make and model of their car. Write down the names and contact information of any witnesses and, if police respond, the officer's name and the police report or case number. Photographing the damage and writing a short summary of exactly what happened while it is fresh creates a contemporaneous record that becomes the foundation of the claim.

## Notify Your Insurer With Your Policy Details Ready

Once you are safe, report the claim promptly, and have your own information at hand. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners advises calling [the phone number on your proof-of-insurance card as soon as possible](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-filing-auto-claim) to open the claim. The Insurance Information Institute similarly stresses reaching your insurer quickly, even from the scene, regardless of who was at fault.

To open the claim smoothly, keep these ready: your policy number, the date, time, and location of the incident, your written account of what happened, and the documentation and contact details you gathered at the scene. From there the insurer takes over the assessment. As the NAIC describes it, the company will assign a claims adjuster to [assess the damage and determine the payment](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-filing-auto-claim), so the more complete your handoff, the faster that assessment moves.

## Documents That Vary by Claim Type

Beyond the core paperwork, each kind of claim calls for a few specific items.

For a collision, the police report, the scene and damage photos, the other driver's insurance information, and one or more repair estimates are what the adjuster works from. If fault is contested, witness statements and any dashcam footage carry real weight.

For a theft or vandalism claim, which runs through comprehensive coverage, a police report and case number are essential and usually required before the insurer will pay. For a stolen car, the insurer often asks for all sets of keys and your loan or lease paperwork as well. Our guide on [how to file a theft or vandalism claim](/blogs/how-to-file-a-theft-or-vandalism-claim) covers that process in full.

For a weather or animal loss, also comprehensive, photograph the damage right away and note the date and conditions, since hail, flood, and falling-object claims turn on when and how the damage occurred.

For an injury claim, keep every medical record and bill, documentation of any time missed from work, and a running log of out-of-pocket costs tied to the accident. These build the case for what the injury actually cost you.

## Keep a Record of Every Conversation

The most overlooked document in a claim is the paper trail of the claim itself. Insurers move through many hands, and a clean log of who said what protects you if anything is later disputed. The Insurance Information Institute is explicit about this, recommending you keep [thorough and organized records of anything related to the claim, including the names and phone numbers of everyone you speak with at your insurer and copies of any bills related to the accident](https://www.iii.org/article/how-to-file-an-auto-insurance-claim).

In practice that means writing down the name and title of every adjuster or representative you speak to, the date of each call, and what was said or promised. Keep copies of every form you submit and every estimate and bill. Note your claim number the moment you receive it and put it on everything. This record costs nothing to keep and becomes the backbone of any appeal if the claim is denied or underpaid, which our guide on [how to appeal a denied claim](/blogs/how-to-appeal-a-denied-auto-insurance-claim) relies on directly.

## Mind the Deadlines and the Deductible

Two practical points shape how and when you file. First, do not wait. Most policies expect prompt notice, and a long delay can give the insurer grounds to question the claim. The Insurance Information Institute recommends confirming with your insurer whether [your policy contains a time limit for filing claims and submitting bills](https://www.iii.org/article/how-to-file-an-auto-insurance-claim), so you know your window before it closes.

Second, remember the deductible when you decide whether to file at all. On a comprehensive or collision claim, you pay your deductible before coverage applies. The Texas Department of Insurance gives the basic mechanic: the insurer will [deduct your deductible from the claim amount](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb020.html) and pay the rest. If a repair estimate is close to your deductible, the payout may be small enough that filing is not worth it. Gathering an estimate first, before you open a claim, lets you make that call with real numbers. Many of the missteps that quietly shrink a payout come down to thin documentation or filing the wrong small claims, which our guide on [common mistakes people make when filing a claim](/blogs/common-mistakes-when-filing-a-claim) covers in detail.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do I need to file a car insurance claim?

At minimum you need your policy number, the date, time, and location of the incident, an account of what happened, the other driver's insurance and contact information, photos of the damage and scene, and a police report or case number if one exists. Repair estimates and, for injuries, medical bills round out the file.

### Do I need a police report to file a claim?

Not always for a minor collision, but it helps, and for theft or vandalism a police report and case number are usually required before the insurer will pay. A report creates an official, timestamped record of the loss and its cause, so file one whenever police will take it and keep the case number.

### How soon do I have to file a car insurance claim?

As soon as possible. Most policies require prompt notice, and a long delay can give the insurer grounds to dispute the claim. Check your policy for any stated time limit on filing claims and submitting bills, and report the incident quickly even if you are still gathering documents.

### Should I get a repair estimate before filing a claim?

It often helps. An estimate tells you whether the damage is worth claiming once your deductible is subtracted, since a repair close to your deductible may not be worth filing. It also gives the adjuster a starting figure and speeds the assessment.

### What records should I keep during the claim?

Keep the names, titles, and phone numbers of everyone you speak with at the insurer, the date and substance of each call, your claim number, and copies of every form, estimate, and bill. This log protects you if the claim is delayed, denied, or underpaid and becomes the basis of any appeal.

## Key takeaways

- The core of any claim is proof of what happened, proof of the damage, your policy details, and a record of everyone you deal with.
- Document the scene first: photos from several angles, the other driver's insurance and contact details, witnesses, and any police report number.
- Report the claim promptly with your policy number and account ready, since the adjuster's assessment starts from what you hand over.
- The required documents shift by claim type, with theft and vandalism needing a police report and often all keys and loan paperwork.
- Keep a written log of every call, form, estimate, and bill, because it is the backbone of any later appeal.
- Watch your policy's time limit and weigh a repair estimate against your deductible before deciding to file.
