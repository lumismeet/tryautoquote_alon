If your car is stolen or vandalized, the claim goes through the comprehensive part of your policy, not collision or liability, so you can only file if you carry comprehensive coverage. The process is the same in outline for both: report it to the police and get a report number, notify your insurer promptly, document everything, and let the adjuster assess the loss. From there the payout is the cost to repair the damage, or your car's actual cash value if it is stolen and not recovered or is damaged beyond repair, minus your comprehensive deductible. The details are where claims get slowed down or shortchanged, so here is exactly how to file a theft or vandalism claim and get it paid.

## Theft and Vandalism Are Comprehensive Claims

Both theft and vandalism fall under comprehensive coverage, which handles damage from events other than a collision. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners defines comprehensive as coverage that [pays for damage to your car that's not caused by a collision, like theft, hail, windstorm, flood, fire, or impact by an animal](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-auto-insurance-coverage). The Insurance Information Institute describes it the same way, as coverage [against theft and damage caused by an incident other than a collision, such as fire, flood, vandalism, hail, falling rocks or trees and other hazards](https://www.iii.org/article/auto-insurance-basics-understanding-your-coverage).

That single fact drives everything about these claims. Because comprehensive is optional coverage, a driver who carries liability only has no coverage for a stolen or vandalized car and cannot file at all. If you finance or lease, you almost certainly carry comprehensive, because lenders require it. If you own the car outright and dropped comprehensive to save money, a theft or vandalism loss comes out of your own pocket. So the first thing to confirm, before anything else, is that comprehensive is on your policy. If you want the full picture of what this coverage does and does not include, our explainer on [what comprehensive coverage actually covers](/blogs/what-comprehensive-coverage-covers) breaks it down.

## What Comprehensive Pays, and What It Doesn't

Comprehensive covers the vehicle and its permanently attached parts. In a theft, that means the car itself, and if the car is recovered stripped, parts like the wheels, catalytic converter, or airbags. In vandalism, it means the physical damage to the car: keyed paint, slashed tires, broken windows and mirrors, dents, or a smashed body panel. Broken glass is a common vandalism claim, and many policies handle glass with a reduced deductible or none at all.

What comprehensive does not cover is the personal property inside the car. A stolen laptop, a bag, tools, or a car seat taken in a break-in are generally not covered by your auto policy; those losses typically fall under a homeowners or renters policy instead. It also does not cover aftermarket equipment beyond a small built-in limit unless you specifically added coverage for it. Knowing this in advance saves a frustrating surprise, because people often assume everything lost in a car theft is an auto claim when the belongings and the vehicle are handled by two different policies.

## How to File a Theft Claim, Step by Step

Speed and documentation matter more in a theft claim than almost any other, because the police and your insurer both act on what you report early.

Start with the police. Report the theft to law enforcement immediately and get the official report and case number, because your insurer will require it before paying a total-theft claim. Have your vehicle details ready: the year, make, model, color, license plate, and vehicle identification number, along with when and where you last saw the car. A prompt, detailed police report is the backbone of the claim and also aids any chance of recovering the vehicle.

Next, notify your insurer as soon as possible. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners advises calling [the phone number on your proof-of-insurance card as soon as possible](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-filing-auto-claim) to open the claim. The insurer will assign an adjuster, who, as the same guidance notes, will [assess the damage and determine the payment](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-filing-auto-claim). Provide the police report number, your account of what happened, and any documentation the adjuster requests, which for a stolen car often includes all sets of keys and your loan or lease information.

Then keep organized records. The Insurance Information Institute recommends keeping [thorough and organized records of anything related to the claim, including the names and phone numbers of everyone you speak with at your insurer](https://www.iii.org/article/how-do-i-file-claim). Note that with a stolen car, insurers usually allow a waiting period to see whether the vehicle is recovered before settling it as a total loss, since a car found quickly changes the claim from a total theft to a repair.

## How to File a Vandalism Claim, Step by Step

A vandalism claim follows the same shape but centers on documenting damage rather than a missing car.

Photograph everything before you touch or clean anything. Take wide shots showing the whole car and its surroundings and close shots of every damaged area: the scratched paint, the broken glass, the slashed tire, the dent. If the vandalism happened in a parking lot or on a street, capture the location and anything nearby that provides context.

File a police report for the vandalism. Even when no suspect is known, an official report creates a timestamped record of the damage and its cause, which supports the claim and is often required by the insurer. Report the incident to your insurer promptly, provide the photos and the police report, and let the adjuster inspect the car and estimate the repair. As with any claim, keep copies of the estimate, receipts, and every communication.

For both theft and vandalism, resist the urge to repair or clean up before the adjuster has documented the loss, and avoid downplaying the damage. Many of the missteps that quietly shrink a payout are the same ones that trip people up in any claim, which our guide on [common mistakes people make when filing a claim](/blogs/common-mistakes-when-filing-a-claim) covers in detail.

## How the Payout Works

Once the adjuster has assessed the loss, two numbers decide what you receive: your deductible and, for a total loss, your car's actual cash value.

For repairable vandalism, comprehensive pays the cost to repair the damage minus your comprehensive deductible. The Texas Department of Insurance gives the basic mechanic: on a claim, the insurer will [deduct your deductible from the claim amount](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb020.html) and pay the rest. If a repair estimate is small and close to your deductible, it may not be worth filing at all.

For a stolen car that is never recovered, or one recovered so badly damaged that repair costs approach its value, the insurer totals it and pays the actual cash value, which the Texas Department of Insurance defines as [the cost to replace your car, minus depreciation](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb020.html), again minus your deductible. That means the payout reflects your car's depreciated market value, not what you paid for it. If you still owe more on a loan or lease than that value, gap insurance covers the difference, and if you disagree with the valuation, you can document comparable vehicles and invoke your policy's appraisal clause. Our explainer on [how actual cash value works](/blogs/actual-cash-value-vs-replacement-cost) covers how that number is set and how to push back on a low offer.

## What Can Delay or Reduce a Theft Claim

Theft claims draw more scrutiny than most, because staged thefts are a known form of fraud, so a clean, consistent, well-documented claim moves faster. Delays and reductions usually trace to a few avoidable things: reporting the theft to police or the insurer late, being unable to produce all sets of keys, inconsistencies between your account and the evidence, or gaps in your documentation. Providing the police report number promptly, handing over the keys and loan paperwork the adjuster asks for, and keeping your story accurate and consistent are what keep the claim on track.

Remember too that comprehensive does not automatically include a rental car while your claim is processed. Rental reimbursement is a separate optional coverage, so if you want a loaner while a stolen car is missing or a vandalized one is in the shop, check whether you carry it. And keep your expectations aligned with the coverage: comprehensive restores the car or its value, minus the deductible, but it does not replace the personal items taken with it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does insurance cover a stolen car?

Yes, if you carry comprehensive coverage. Theft is a comprehensive claim, so a driver with liability only has no coverage for a stolen car. If the car is not recovered, comprehensive pays its actual cash value minus your deductible. If it is recovered with damage, comprehensive pays to repair it minus the deductible.

### Is vandalism covered by car insurance?

Yes, vandalism is covered under comprehensive coverage. That includes keyed paint, slashed tires, broken windows, and similar damage. You pay your comprehensive deductible, and the insurer covers the rest of the repair. A driver without comprehensive coverage would have to pay for vandalism damage out of pocket.

### Should I file a police report for theft or vandalism?

Yes. A police report and case number create an official, timestamped record of the loss and its cause, and insurers typically require one before paying a theft or vandalism claim. Report it promptly and have your vehicle details ready, since a fast, detailed report also helps any chance of recovering a stolen car.

### Does comprehensive cover items stolen from inside my car?

Generally no. Comprehensive covers the vehicle and its attached parts, but personal belongings taken from inside the car, like a laptop or bag, usually fall under a homeowners or renters policy instead. Check that policy for coverage on stolen personal items.

### Will a theft or vandalism claim raise my rates?

It can, though comprehensive claims often affect rates less than at-fault accidents. The impact depends on your insurer, your claim history, and your state. Weigh the repair cost against your deductible before filing, since a small vandalism repair close to your deductible may not be worth a claim.

## Key takeaways

- Theft and vandalism are comprehensive claims, so you can only file if you carry comprehensive coverage.
- Comprehensive covers the vehicle and its attached parts, not personal belongings inside, which fall under home or renters insurance.
- For both, report to police and get a case number, notify your insurer promptly, and document everything before repairs.
- Vandalism pays the repair cost minus your deductible; a stolen or destroyed car pays actual cash value minus your deductible.
- Insurers usually allow a waiting period on a stolen car to see if it is recovered before settling it as a total loss.
- Theft claims get extra scrutiny, so prompt reporting, all sets of keys, and consistent documentation keep the claim moving.
