Choosing a car insurance company on price alone is the most common and costly mistake, because the point of insurance is that the company actually pays when you have a claim. The warning signs worth checking before you buy are a quote that looks far too good to be true, weak financial strength ratings, a high volume of consumer complaints relative to the company's size, no license to operate in your state, and a reputation for slow or lowball claims handling. All of these are things you can verify for free in a few minutes using independent ratings and your state insurance department. Here are the red flags to watch for and exactly how to check each one before you hand over a premium.

## A Price That Looks Too Good to Be True

The first red flag is the one that feels like a win: a quote dramatically lower than everyone else's. A modest difference is normal, since insurers weigh risk differently, but a price far below every other quote for the same coverage deserves suspicion rather than celebration. It can signal stripped-down coverage you did not notice, a company that competes on price and then underpays or slow-walks claims, a financially shaky insurer, or a teaser rate that jumps at the first renewal.

The fix is to make sure you are comparing the same thing. Confirm the coverage types, limits, and deductibles match across every quote, because a low price often hides lower limits or missing coverages. Our guide on [how to compare auto insurance quotes the right way](/blogs/how-to-compare-auto-insurance) walks through setting up an apples-to-apples comparison so a suspiciously low number reveals itself as either a genuine deal or a warning.

## Weak Financial Strength Ratings

An insurer is only as good as its ability to pay claims, especially large ones after a catastrophe when many policyholders file at once. That ability is measured by independent financial strength ratings, and a weak or missing rating is a serious red flag. The Insurance Information Institute notes that five independent agencies, A.M. Best, Fitch, Kroll Bond Rating Agency (KBRA), Moody's, and Standard and Poor's, [rate the financial strength of insurance companies](https://www.iii.org/article/how-to-assess-the-financial-strength-of-an-insurance-company).

Do not rely on a single rating, because the agencies do not always agree. The Insurance Information Institute advises that [you should consider a company's rating from two or more agencies before judging whether to buy or keep a policy from that company](https://www.iii.org/article/how-to-assess-the-financial-strength-of-an-insurance-company). Checking two ratings takes minutes and tells you whether the company behind the low quote can actually stand behind a big claim.

## A High Complaint Record

Financial strength tells you whether a company can pay; its complaint record tells you how it treats the people it pays. Every state tracks consumer complaints, and the National Association of Insurance Commissioners publishes searchable data. Through it you can view [the insurance carrier's financial condition, how long it has been in business, and its complaints over the past three years](https://content.naic.org/article/how-file-complaint-and-research-complaints-against-insurance-carriers).

A high number of complaints relative to a company's size, often expressed as a complaint index, is a warning that claims may be delayed, disputed, or underpaid. But read it in context, and do not stop at one number. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners cautions that [you should not use just one factor to decide which insurance company to use](https://content.naic.org/article/how-file-complaint-and-research-complaints-against-insurance-carriers), advising instead that [you should compare the complaints, financials, and direct premiums written of several companies](https://content.naic.org/article/how-file-complaint-and-research-complaints-against-insurance-carriers). A company with many complaints but a poor payment reputation is a clear one to avoid.

## No License to Operate in Your State

This is the reddest flag of all: an insurer must be licensed in your state to sell you a policy there, and one that is not licensed may be a scam or an unregulated operation that leaves you unprotected. Legitimate insurers are licensed and regulated by the state insurance department, which is also the body that would help you if the company failed to pay a valid claim.

Verifying this is simple and free. Your state insurance department maintains a lookup for licensed companies and agents, and the NAIC's consumer tools let you confirm a company's licensing status along with its financial and complaint history. If you cannot confirm that a company, or the agent selling for it, is licensed in your state, do not buy the policy. An unlicensed insurer is not a bargain at any price.

## A Poor Claims Reputation

Because a policy is only worth what it pays when you need it, how a company handles claims is the whole game. Slow payments, routine lowball offers, denied claims that should be covered, and hard-to-reach adjusters are the practical red flags, and they show up in the complaint data above as well as in a company's reputation among people who have actually filed. Pay particular attention to complaints specifically about claims handling rather than billing or sales.

If you are weighing an insurer, it is worth knowing how you would push back if a claim went wrong, since even good companies make mistakes. Our guide on [how to appeal a denied auto insurance claim](/blogs/how-to-appeal-a-denied-auto-insurance-claim) covers that process, but the better move is to avoid a company with a pattern of bad claims handling in the first place.

## Other Warning Signs

A few softer red flags are worth noting during the sales process itself. High-pressure tactics, a refusal to put coverage details or promises in writing, vague or evasive answers to direct questions about what is and is not covered, and pricing that seems designed to be hard to compare all suggest a company or agent you may not want to rely on later. So does a history of steep, unexplained rate increases at renewal, which our guide on [why your car insurance went up](/blogs/why-your-car-insurance-went-up) can help you make sense of.

None of these alone is proof of a bad insurer, but together with the checks above they build a clear picture. The overall lesson is to weigh several factors together, financial strength, complaints, licensing, claims reputation, and a sensible price, rather than deciding on any single one. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I check if an insurance company is reliable?

Check its financial strength rating from two or more independent agencies, review its consumer complaint record through the NAIC or your state insurance department, and confirm it is licensed in your state. Weigh those factors together with the price rather than deciding on any single one. All of these checks are free and take only a few minutes.

### Is the cheapest car insurance quote a bad sign?

Not necessarily, but a quote far below every other for the same coverage is worth scrutinizing. It can mean lower limits or missing coverage, a company that underpays claims, financial weakness, or a rate that jumps at renewal. Confirm the coverage matches exactly and check the company's ratings and complaints before choosing on price.

### What is a complaint index and where do I find it?

A complaint index compares the number of consumer complaints against a company with other companies selling the same type of insurance, so a higher index means relatively more complaints. You can find complaint data, along with a company's financial condition and years in business, through the NAIC's consumer tools and your state insurance department.

### How do I know if an insurer is licensed in my state?

Your state insurance department maintains a free lookup for licensed insurance companies and agents, and the NAIC's consumer tools show licensing status as well. If you cannot confirm a company or agent is licensed to operate in your state, do not buy the policy, since an unlicensed insurer may be a scam.

### Should I choose an insurer based on financial strength alone?

No. Financial strength tells you whether a company can pay claims, but not how well it treats claimants. Combine it with the company's complaint record, its licensing status, its claims reputation, and a fair price. Relying on any single factor, including a strong rating, can still leave you with a poor experience.

## Key takeaways

- Do not choose an insurer on price alone, since the point of insurance is that the company pays when you file a claim.
- Treat a quote far below all others as a warning to check coverage, ratings, and complaints, not as an automatic win.
- Check financial strength ratings from two or more independent agencies before buying or renewing.
- Review the company's complaint record through the NAIC or your state insurance department, focusing on claims-handling complaints.
- Confirm the insurer is licensed in your state, since an unlicensed company may be a scam and leaves you unprotected.
- Weigh financial strength, complaints, licensing, claims reputation, and price together rather than relying on any one factor.
