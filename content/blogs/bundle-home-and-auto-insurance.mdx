Yes, you can almost always bundle your home and auto insurance with one company, and doing so usually earns a discount, commonly in the range of 5 to 15 percent off your premium. Insurers reward it because keeping more of your business makes you a more valuable, longer-staying customer. The catch is that a bundle is only a deal if the combined price is lower than buying each policy from the cheapest separate insurer, and that is not guaranteed. Bundling also brings smaller conveniences like one renewal date and a single company to call after a loss. Here is how the discount works, what it typically combines, and how to tell whether it is genuinely saving you money.

## How Bundling Works

Bundling, sometimes called a multi-policy discount, simply means buying two or more types of insurance from the same company. The most common pairing is home and auto, but it can also mean combining auto with renters, condo, or liability coverage, or insuring more than one vehicle with one carrier. In exchange, the insurer takes a percentage off your premiums.

The discount is real and often meaningful. The Insurance Information Institute notes that [some companies that sell homeowners, auto and liability coverage will take 5 to 15 percent off your premium if you buy two or more policies from them](https://www.iii.org/article/12-ways-to-lower-your-homeowners-insurance-costs). The same principle applies from the auto side: the Institute lists bundling among its ways to cut car insurance costs, noting that [many insurers will give you a break if you buy two or more types of insurance](https://www.iii.org/article/nine-ways-to-lower-your-auto-insurance-costs). Insurers offer this because a customer with several policies is more likely to stay, and retention is worth paying for.

## Why Insurers Reward It

Understanding why the discount exists helps you judge how far to trust it. A customer who holds only one policy is easy to lose to a competitor at renewal. A customer with home and auto together is stickier: switching means moving two policies, comparing more coverage, and doing more paperwork, so they tend to stay longer. That lower churn is valuable to the insurer, and the multi-policy discount is how they share a bit of that value to win and keep your whole account.

That same stickiness is the thing to watch. The discount is designed partly to discourage you from shopping around, and over time a bundled premium can drift upward while you assume you are still getting the best deal because of the discount. The reward is genuine, but it works best when you keep verifying it rather than setting it and forgetting it.

## When Bundling Actually Saves You Money

A discount off one company's price is not the same as the lowest price available. This is the single most important thing to understand about bundling. A 10 percent discount on a premium that was high to begin with can still cost more than two competitively priced standalone policies. The Insurance Information Institute makes the point directly, advising that after a bundling offer you should [make certain this combined price is lower than buying the different coverages from different companies](https://www.iii.org/article/12-ways-to-lower-your-homeowners-insurance-costs).

So treat the bundle as one quote among several, not as the automatic answer. Get the bundled price from insurers that offer it, then separately price your auto policy and your home policy with the best specialist insurer for each, and compare the totals. Sometimes the bundle wins comfortably. Sometimes a standalone auto policy from a company that prices your profile well, paired with a separate home policy, beats it. The only way to know is to run both. ## The Non-Price Benefits

Money aside, bundling carries a few conveniences that some drivers value. You deal with one company, one account login, and often a single renewal date, which is simpler to manage. After a loss that touches both policies, such as a storm that damages your house and your car, having one insurer can streamline the claims. And a bundled customer sometimes gets a bit more leniency, though this varies and should never be assumed.

These are worth something, but they are secondary. If two setups are close in price, the convenience of a bundle can reasonably tip the decision. If the bundle costs noticeably more, the convenience rarely justifies the difference. Keep price as the primary test and treat convenience as the tiebreaker.

## How to Decide

Start by getting a bundled quote from one or two insurers that offer home and auto together. Then price each policy separately with the strongest insurer for each, using identical coverage so the comparison is fair. Add up the standalone totals and put them next to the bundled total.

If the bundle is cheaper or within a small margin, take it and enjoy the simplicity. If separate policies clearly win, buy them separately and revisit the math at renewal. Either way, recheck it every year or two, because both bundled and standalone prices move, and today's best option is not permanent. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much can you save by bundling home and auto insurance?

The discount commonly runs from 5 to 15 percent off your premiums, according to the Insurance Information Institute, though the exact figure varies by insurer, state, and the policies involved. The real saving depends on the starting price, so a discount on an already high premium may still cost more than two competitively priced separate policies.

### Is bundling home and auto always cheaper?

No. Bundling usually earns a discount, but a discounted price is not automatically the lowest price. You have to compare the combined bundled price against buying each policy from the cheapest separate insurer. Sometimes the bundle wins, and sometimes two well-priced standalone policies beat it.

### What policies can I bundle together?

The most common bundle is home and auto, but you can also combine auto with renters, condo, or liability coverage, or insure multiple vehicles with one company. Any pairing of two or more policies with the same insurer typically qualifies for a multi-policy discount.

### Should I bundle even if I rent instead of own?

Often yes. Renters insurance is inexpensive, and bundling it with your auto policy can still earn a multi-policy discount that lowers your car insurance while adding coverage for your belongings. Compare the bundled total against separate policies to confirm it saves money.

### Does bundling make it harder to switch insurers later?

It can add friction, since switching means moving two policies rather than one, which is part of why insurers offer the discount. That is a reason to recheck your bundled price against the market every year or two, so the convenience does not quietly cost you money over time.

## Key takeaways

- Bundling means buying two or more policies, most often home and auto, from one insurer in exchange for a discount.
- The multi-policy discount commonly runs 5 to 15 percent, and insurers offer it because bundled customers tend to stay longer.
- A bundle only saves money if the combined price beats buying each policy from the cheapest separate insurer, so always compare.
- Treat the bundle as one quote among several, pricing standalone auto and home policies with the best insurer for each.
- Non-price perks like one renewal date and simpler claims are real but secondary, best used as a tiebreaker on close prices.
- Recheck the math every year or two, since both bundled and standalone prices drift over time.
