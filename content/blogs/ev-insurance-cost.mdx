## Do Electric Vehicles Cost More to Insure?

Yes, on average. Multiple 2026 industry studies put the gap anywhere from about 18 percent to nearly 50 percent higher than comparable gas-powered vehicles, with the exact number depending heavily on the model, model year, and which study's methodology is used.

One widely cited 2026 analysis puts the average full-coverage EV premium at $3,159 a year across all model years, compared to $2,218 for gas-powered cars, a 42 percent gap. But that gap narrows significantly when comparing only newer vehicles: limited to 2024-and-newer models, the difference drops to around 18 percent, since modern gas cars increasingly carry the same advanced safety technology that used to be an EV-specific advantage. Other studies that weight the sample differently, or include more luxury EVs, report gaps closer to 49 percent. The honest takeaway is that the size of the gap depends heavily on which EV is being compared to which gas car, not a single fixed number that applies to every driver.

## Why Do EVs Cost More to Insure?

The core reason isn't that EVs get into more accidents. It's that when something does go wrong, the claim tends to cost more to resolve. Four factors drive most of that gap.

**Proprietary parts with few aftermarket alternatives.** Many EV components, especially battery packs, sensors, and drive units, are specific to the manufacturer. The aftermarket parts ecosystem that keeps gas-car repair costs down over time largely doesn't exist yet for EVs, so insurers pay closer to full manufacturer pricing on repairs.

**A shortage of certified repair technicians.** EV repairs require technicians trained and certified on high-voltage systems. Fewer body shops have that training and equipment, which limits competition and keeps labor rates elevated. One industry analysis found average EV repair time running around 15.6 days compared to 12.7 days for gas vehicles, and longer repair times mean longer rental car reimbursement periods for insurers to cover.

**Expensive, hard-to-assess battery packs.** Replacing a battery pack can run anywhere from around $6,000 for a smaller pack to well over $15,000 for a larger one, depending on the model. Because it can be difficult to fully assess battery damage after a crash, insurers sometimes total an EV after an accident that would have been a straightforward repair on a comparable gas car, simply because inspecting or replacing the pack approaches the value of the vehicle itself.

**Higher vehicle values overall.** EVs still carry a higher average purchase price than gas cars, in part because the EV lineup still skews toward luxury models. A higher replacement value [directly increases what comprehensive and collision coverage have to pay out](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-price-my-auto-insurance-policy) if the car is stolen or totaled.

## Do EVs Actually Get Into More Accidents?

No, and this is the detail most people get backwards. Research from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety and its data affiliate, comparing electric and gas versions of the same model, found that [EVs actually had fewer claims for property damage, collision, bodily injury, and medical payments](https://www.iihs.org/media/ca2618fc-c875-4246-8a9f-5977f3b702f6/Ewxm_A/HLDI%20Research/Bulletins/hldi_bulletin_37-25.pdf) than their gas counterparts. The property damage claims that did occur, however, were more expensive on average.

In other words, EVs aren't priced higher because they're driven more recklessly or crash more often. They're priced higher because the claims that do happen cost more to resolve, for the repair and battery reasons above. That's a meaningfully different problem than a risky-driver problem, and it's one that eases over time as repair infrastructure matures rather than one tied to driving behavior.

## Does Every EV Cost More to Insure Than Every Gas Car?

No. The averages hide enormous variation within the EV category itself, largely because the EV market still leans luxury. Mainstream, non-luxury EVs from brands like Hyundai, Kia, Toyota, Chevrolet, and Volkswagen commonly insure close to, or even below, the broader EV average, sometimes landing near what a comparable gas vehicle would cost.

At the low end, one 2026 analysis found the Chevrolet Silverado EV insuring for around $1,947 a year for full coverage, close to a comparable gas truck. At the high end, luxury and performance EVs push the average up dramatically: all five Tesla models in that same analysis exceeded $2,800 a year for full coverage, and the Audi SQ8 e-tron reached over $10,000 a year, the highest figure in the dataset. The lesson is that "EV" isn't really the variable that matters most. Vehicle value, repair complexity, and how mainstream or specialized the model is matter more than the powertrain alone.

## Is the EV Insurance Gap Shrinking?

Yes, gradually. As more mainstream, non-luxury EVs enter the market and as certified repair networks expand, the pricing gap between EVs and comparable gas cars has been narrowing year over year, particularly for newer model years. Broad adoption of advanced driver-assistance technology across gas vehicles is also part of the shift: technology that used to be an EV-specific rating factor is now common across new vehicles generally, which shrinks the relative gap rather than EVs necessarily getting cheaper to insure in absolute terms.

That said, some data from 2026 shows EV insurance costs still rising faster year-over-year than gas-vehicle insurance costs in raw dollar terms, even as the percentage gap between newer models narrows. The trend is real, but it isn't a straight line, and it's worth checking current rates directly rather than assuming the gap has already closed for a specific model.

## Does the EV Insurance Gap Vary by State?

Significantly. States with the largest EV-versus-gas insurance gaps tend to fall into one of two categories: dense coastal markets with higher baseline collision and repair costs generally, or less densely populated regions where a thin certified-repair network and higher rates of severe weather both push EV-specific risk up further than the national pattern would suggest.

Local EV adoption rates play a role too. In states or regions where EVs remain relatively uncommon, the local repair network has less incentive to build out high-voltage certification and specialized equipment, which keeps repair times and costs elevated in exactly the areas where an EV owner is most likely to need that infrastructure. As adoption climbs in a given state, that local gap tends to close faster than the national average does, since certified shops and dedicated EV parts suppliers become easier to support financially in that market.

## Does Insurance Offset the Rest of an EV's Lower Running Costs?

Partially, and it's worth looking at the full picture rather than the insurance line item in isolation. EVs typically cost meaningfully less to fuel than a comparable gas car, since home charging is generally cheaper per mile than gasoline, and they also tend to save on maintenance, since there's no oil changes, fewer moving parts in the drivetrain, and less brake wear thanks to regenerative braking.

Some total-cost-of-ownership estimates comparing a typical EV to a similar gas vehicle over a ten-year period, factoring in fuel, maintenance, and insurance together, find the higher insurance cost only partially offsets the fuel and maintenance savings, rather than erasing them entirely. The exact math depends heavily on local electricity and gas prices, annual mileage, and the specific vehicles being compared, but insurance alone rarely flips an EV from a cheaper overall ownership cost to a more expensive one. It's one line item in a larger comparison, not the deciding factor by itself.

## How Can You Lower the Cost of Insuring an EV?

**Shop multiple carriers.** Pricing for EVs varies more between insurers than it does for most gas vehicles, since carriers are still refining how they model EV-specific risk. Getting quotes from at least three or four carriers, including any that specifically advertise EV or usage-based programs, is more likely to surface a meaningfully better rate for an EV than for an equivalent gas car.

**Use a telematics or usage-based insurance program.** Connected EVs make it easy for an insurer to verify low mileage and safe driving habits directly through the vehicle's own data, often without a separate plug-in device. For EV owners who drive relatively few miles or drive carefully, this can meaningfully close the pricing gap.

**Raise the deductible if it's affordable.** Increasing a collision or comprehensive deductible by $250 to $500 can noticeably reduce the premium, the same way it would on any vehicle, though it means covering more out of pocket if a claim happens.

**Ask about safety feature discounts.** Many EVs come equipped with automatic emergency braking, lane-keeping assistance, and other advanced safety technology. Not every insurer applies these discounts automatically, so it's worth confirming the specific features are on file with the policy.

**Choose a mainstream model over a luxury one, where that choice is available.** Since so much of the EV insurance gap is driven by luxury pricing and specialized parts rather than the electric powertrain itself, a Hyundai, Kia, Toyota, or Chevrolet EV commonly insures closer to a gas-car rate than a Tesla, Audi, or Rivian would.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are Teslas more expensive to insure than other EVs?

Generally, yes. Tesla models consistently rank among the more expensive EVs to insure in industry rate studies, driven by a combination of vehicle value, repair costs, and Tesla's own parts and service network.

### Do EV owners get a discount just for driving an electric car?

Not universally. Some insurers offer a [green vehicle or hybrid/EV-specific discount](https://www.iii.org/article/green-insurance), but many major carriers do not, applying the same standard discounts (bundling, good driver, good student, telematics) that any driver would qualify for regardless of powertrain.

### Does a home charging station affect insurance?

Not typically for the vehicle's auto policy. A home charger is usually a homeowners insurance consideration rather than an auto insurance one, since it's a fixed installation rather than part of the vehicle.

### Is it cheaper to insure a used EV than a new one?

Often, yes, since a lower actual cash value reduces the comprehensive and collision payout exposure, similar to how any used vehicle typically costs less to insure than the same model new. Battery health and remaining warranty coverage can still affect the rate on a used EV specifically.

### Does insurance cost more for a leased EV?

Leasing itself doesn't change the underlying rate much, but leases require full coverage and often cap your deductible, which can push the premium higher than a liability-only policy on the same car. The vehicle's value and repair costs still drive most of the difference.

### Will my EV get cheaper to insure the longer I own it?

Usually yes, since the car's value drops each year, which lowers the collision and comprehensive portion of the premium. Expanding certified repair networks and a growing number of mainstream models are also narrowing the EV-versus-gas gap over time.

## The Bottom Line

The insurance premium is a real, often underestimated cost of EV ownership, and it's driven almost entirely by repair economics rather than how EVs are actually driven. The gap is narrowing as the market matures and mainstream models multiply, but it hasn't closed yet, and it varies enormously by model. Comparing insurance quotes on specific EV models before buying, not just researching the purchase price and range, is the most reliable way to avoid a premium surprise after the fact.

## Key takeaways

- EVs commonly cost 18 to 49 percent more to insure than comparable gas cars, with the gap narrowing to roughly 18 percent when comparing only 2024-and-newer models.
- The higher cost is driven by repair economics, not accident frequency. Research comparing electric and gas versions of the same model found EVs actually have fewer claims, just more expensive ones.
- Proprietary parts, a shortage of certified high-voltage technicians, expensive battery packs, and higher overall vehicle value are the four main drivers of the cost gap.
- Mainstream, non-luxury EVs from brands like Hyundai, Kia, Toyota, and Chevrolet often insure close to gas-car rates. Luxury and performance EVs like Tesla, Audi e-tron, and Rivian drive most of the category's higher average.
- The gap is shrinking over time as repair networks expand and safety technology becomes standard across gas vehicles too, though it hasn't closed.
- Shopping multiple carriers, using a telematics program, raising the deductible, and confirming safety feature discounts are the most effective ways to lower an EV's premium.
