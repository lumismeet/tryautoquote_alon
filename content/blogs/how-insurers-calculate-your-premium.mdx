Your car insurance premium is the insurer's estimate of how likely you are to file a claim and how expensive that claim is likely to be, translated into a price. It is built from a handful of factors: your driving record, your age and experience, the car you drive, where you live, how much you drive, your credit history in most states, and the coverage and deductible you pick. Some of these you cannot change, like your age or your ZIP code, and some you fully control, like your deductible and your record going forward. Because each company weighs these factors differently, the same driver can get very different prices, which is exactly why shopping matters. Here is how each piece feeds into the number you pay.

## What a Premium Actually Represents

Before the individual factors, it helps to understand what the price is measuring. An insurer is not judging you personally; it is predicting risk. Every factor in your quote is there because, across millions of policies, it correlates with how often drivers file claims and how large those claims run. Your premium is essentially that predicted cost, plus the insurer's expenses and margin.

That framing explains two things. First, why factors that feel unrelated to your driving, like your credit or your ZIP code, still affect your rate: they are statistically predictive even if they are not about skill. Second, why prices vary so much between companies. Each insurer builds its own model and weights the factors differently, so a profile one company prices as high risk another may see as ordinary. The practical takeaway is that no single quote is the true price, which is the whole reason comparison shopping pays off.

## Your Driving Record

The most intuitive factor is your history behind the wheel, and it carries real weight. Insurers treat a clean record as strong evidence you are a lower risk. The Insurance Information Institute puts it plainly: [the better your record, the lower your premium. If you've had accidents or serious traffic violations, it's likely you'll pay more than if you have a clean driving record](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy).

At-fault accidents and moving violations like speeding or a DUI raise your rate, often for three to five years, after which they typically age off. This is one of the factors you most directly control going forward, since every year of clean driving moves you back toward a lower price. Our guide on [how an accident affects your insurance rate](/blogs/how-an-accident-affects-your-insurance-rate) covers how long a single claim tends to follow you and by how much.

## Your Age and Experience

Age is really a proxy for experience, and it is one of the largest factors for the youngest and oldest drivers. Inexperienced drivers crash more often, so they pay more. The Insurance Information Institute notes that [insurers generally charge more if teenagers or young people below age 25 drive your car](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy), which is why adding a teen to a policy raises the premium sharply.

Rates generally fall as drivers gain experience through their twenties and thirties and settle through the middle-age years, sometimes rising again in advanced age. Gender can also play a role where it is permitted, since the Insurance Information Institute observes that statistically [women tend to get into fewer accidents, have fewer driver-under-the-influence accidents (DUIs) and, most importantly, have less serious accidents than men](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy). Age is a factor you cannot change, but understanding it explains why premiums shift at predictable stages of life. If you are adding a young driver, our explainer on [how adding a teen driver affects your premium](/blogs/how-adding-a-teen-driver-affects-your-premium) breaks down that jump.

## The Car You Drive

Two drivers with identical records can pay very different rates purely because of the vehicle, since the car itself carries risk. The Insurance Information Institute explains that [the cost of your car is a major factor in the cost to insure it. Other variables include the likelihood of theft, the cost of repairs, its engine size and the overall safety record of the car](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy).

In practice, an expensive-to-repair luxury car, a frequently stolen model, or a high-powered sports car all cost more to insure, while a common, moderately priced, safety-rated vehicle keeps premiums down. This is a factor you control at the point of purchase, which is why checking insurance costs before buying a car can save more over the life of the vehicle than any single discount. Electric vehicles have their own cost dynamics, which our guide on [what it costs to insure an EV](/blogs/ev-insurance-cost) covers.

## Where You Live

Your location shapes your rate before you ever turn the key, because risk is concentrated geographically. The Insurance Information Institute notes that [due to higher rates of vandalism, theft and accidents, urban drivers pay a higher auto insurance price than those in small towns or rural areas](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy).

Location captures more than just city versus country. Local repair costs, the volume of litigated claims, the prevalence of insurance fraud, and exposure to severe weather all vary by area and feed into the price. This is why moving even a short distance can change your premium, and it is largely outside your control short of relocating. Our deep dive on [how your ZIP code affects your insurance rate](/blogs/how-zip-code-affects-your-insurance-rate) explains how granular this can get.

## How Much You Drive

The more time your car spends on the road, the more chances there are for a claim, so mileage matters. The Insurance Information Institute states that [the more miles you drive, the more chance for accidents so you'll pay more if you drive your car for work, or use it to commute long distances](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy). Drivers who use the car only occasionally, sometimes classified as pleasure use, generally pay less.

This is a factor you partly control, and it is where usage-based programs come in. If you drive few miles or want to be priced on your actual habits rather than averages, a telematics program can reward it directly, as our guide on [telematics and usage-based insurance](/blogs/telematics-usage-based-insurance) explains.

## Your Credit History

In most states, insurers use a credit-based insurance score as one input, because it has proven statistically predictive of claims. The Insurance Information Institute describes it as a tool where [your credit-based insurance score is a statistical tool that predicts the likelihood of your filing a claim and the likely cost of that claim](https://www.iii.org/article/what-determines-the-price-of-an-auto-insurance-policy).

A stronger credit history generally means a lower premium, and the habits that build it, paying bills on time and keeping balances low, help here over months rather than overnight. A few states restrict or prohibit the use of credit in auto pricing, so its weight depends on where you live. Our explainer on [how your credit score affects your car insurance](/blogs/credit-score-insurance) covers how the score is used and how much it can move your rate.

## The Coverage and Deductible You Choose

The final piece is the one you control most directly: what you actually buy. Higher liability limits, adding collision and comprehensive, and lower deductibles all raise the premium, while trimming optional coverage and raising your deductible lowers it.

The deductible is the clearest lever. Because you agree to pay more out of pocket on a claim, a higher deductible lowers your premium. The Texas Department of Insurance describes the mechanic simply: on a claim, the insurer will [deduct your deductible from the claim amount](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb020.html) and pay the rest, so the more you take on, the less the insurer charges up front. The tradeoff is that you must be able to cover that deductible when a claim happens. Our guides on [how to choose your deductible](/blogs/how-to-choose-your-deductible) and [how to lower your car insurance premiums](/blogs/how-to-lower-your-car-insurance-premiums) walk through setting these levers without cutting protection you actually need.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What factors affect my car insurance premium the most?

The heaviest factors for most drivers are driving record, age and experience, the car you drive, and location, followed by mileage, credit in most states, and the coverage and deductible you choose. Their exact weight varies by insurer, which is why the same driver gets different prices from different companies.

### Why is my premium different from someone with the same car and record?

Because insurers weigh factors differently and price on many inputs beyond the car and record, including age, ZIP code, mileage, credit, and coverage choices. Two people can differ on any of those. Each company also builds its own risk model, so identical profiles can still land at different prices.

### Which premium factors can I actually control?

You control your coverage and deductible directly, your driving record going forward, your mileage to some degree, the car you buy, and your credit over time. You cannot change your age or, short of moving, your location. Focusing on the controllable factors is where real savings come from.

### Does my credit really affect my car insurance price?

In most states, yes. Insurers use a credit-based insurance score because it statistically predicts claim likelihood and cost. Better credit generally means a lower premium. A few states restrict or ban the use of credit in auto pricing, so its impact depends on where you live.

### How can I lower my premium once I know the factors?

Target the ones you control: raise your deductible to a level you can afford, keep a clean record, drop coverage that no longer pays off on an older car, protect your credit, and compare quotes since each insurer weighs the factors differently. Comparison shopping is often the single biggest saving.

## Key takeaways

- Your premium is the insurer's prediction of how likely and how costly a claim from you is, turned into a price.
- The main factors are driving record, age and experience, the car, location, mileage, credit in most states, and your coverage and deductible.
- Some factors are fixed, like age and ZIP code, while others are fully in your control, like your deductible and your record going forward.
- Each insurer weighs the factors differently, so the same driver can get very different quotes.
- The vehicle you buy is a major and controllable factor, so check insurance costs before purchase.
- Focus on the levers you control, especially your deductible, your record, and comparison shopping, to bring the price down.
